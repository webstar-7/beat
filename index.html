    1. What a frequency is

    Sound is air pushed back and forth. One full push-and-pull is a cycle. Frequency is how many cycles happen each second, measured in hertz (Hz). 440 Hz means the air moves back and forth 440 times a second. We hear frequency as pitch: more cycles, higher note.

    • Period is the time for one cycle: 1 ÷ frequency. At 100 Hz each cycle lasts 10 ms.
    • Wavelength is how far one cycle stretches in air: 343 m/s ÷ frequency. A 50 Hz bass wave is almost 7 m long, which is why small rooms boom and small speakers can't make deep bass.
    • Octave: doubling the frequency gives the same note one octave higher. 110, 220, 440, 880 Hz are all the note A.
    • Human hearing runs roughly 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz when young; the top end drops with age and loud exposure. Below 20 Hz (infrasound) you feel it more than hear it.

    Open the Frequency Lab, switch on the Tone layer, and slide from 40 Hz to 15,000 Hz. Watch the spectrum and read the facts box: it shows the period, wavelength, nearest note and mix band for any frequency you type.

    2. Sound and energy

    A sound wave carries energy, and two things decide how much:

    • Amplitude (how far the air moves) sets loudness. Loudness is measured in decibels (dB). +6 dB doubles the pressure; about +10 dB sounds roughly twice as loud; +3 dB doubles the power.
    • Frequency: for the same movement of the air, a wave with twice the frequency carries four times the power (power grows with frequency squared). That is why a tweeter barely moves while a woofer pumps visibly to sound equally loud.

    Your ears are not flat. They are most sensitive around 2–5 kHz (the range of a baby's cry) and much less sensitive to deep bass. A 50 Hz tone needs roughly 30 dB more level to sound as loud as 1 kHz at quiet listening levels. The Lab's facts box shows this for any frequency (it uses the standard "A-weighting" curve).

    What this means in a mix: bass eats most of your headroom. The kick and 808 carry far more energy than the hi-hats, so they decide how loud your beat can get before it clips. That is why engineers high-pass (cut the lows from) instruments that don't need bass, and make sure the kick and 808 take turns.

    "Energy" in the feeling sense is real too, but it comes from different things: tempo (a slow pulse near a resting heart rate, 50–70 BPM, calms; 120+ BPM energises), harmony (consonant intervals relax, clashing ones create tension), timbre (soft sine and pad sounds soothe, bright saws excite) and dynamics. You can control every one of these in this app.

    5. How a beat is built

    • BPM (beats per minute) is the tempo. One bar of 4/4 has 4 beats; the sequencer splits each beat into 4 steps (16th notes), so 16 steps = 1 bar.
    • Kick usually lands on beat 1 (step 1). Snare or clap on beats 2 and 4 (steps 5 and 13) gives the backbeat. Hi-hats keep time between them.
    • Swing delays every second 16th a little. 50% is straight (robotic); 55–62% gives the bounce in Afrobeats, Amapiano and boom bap.
    • Velocity is how hard each hit is. Varying hi-hat velocity (loud, soft, medium, soft) is the fastest way to make a pattern feel human.
    • Tune the kick and 808 to the song's key. Set the Key at the top and pick notes marked with • in the 808 lane. An 808 that clashes with the chords sounds muddy even if it's loud.
    • Gain staging: keep the meter peaking around −6 dBFS before your mastering stage. Export at 24-bit / 48 kHz for studio work.

    6. Brainwave beats

    Your brain's electrical activity has rhythms, measured on an EEG in bands: delta (0.5–4 Hz, deep sleep), theta (4–8 Hz, drowsy, deep meditation), alpha (8–13 Hz, relaxed and awake, eyes closed), beta (13–30 Hz, alert, busy thinking) and gamma (30+ Hz, intense focus). These rates are too slow to hear as pitch, so we deliver them as a pulse on an audible tone:

    • Binaural beat: one tone in the left ear, a slightly different one in the right (e.g. 200 Hz and 210 Hz). Your brain hears a 10 Hz wobble that isn't in the air at all. Needs headphones.
    • Monaural beat: both tones mixed in the air, so the wobble is real. Works on speakers.
    • Isochronic tone: one tone switched on and off at the beat rate. The strongest, most obvious pulse.

    What the research says: reviews of binaural-beat studies find small-to-moderate effects on anxiety and some effects on attention and pain perception, but results vary a lot and the idea that your brainwaves simply "lock on" to the beat is still debated. Treat them as a useful relaxation aid, not a guaranteed switch.

    7. Healing, meditation and manifestation frequencies

    Many traditions give special meaning to particular frequencies: the Solfeggio set (174–963 Hz), 432 Hz tuning, 528 Hz "love" frequency, 136.1 Hz "Om", 7.83 Hz Schumann resonance. This app lets you create any of them precisely, so you can explore them yourself.

    To use them well, it helps to know which parts are measured and which are belief:

    • Measured: calm, slow, soft sound lowers heart rate and stress for many people. Music and humming you enjoy can reduce perceived pain and anxiety. Pink noise has been linked to deeper sleep in some studies. 40 Hz light-and-sound stimulation is being researched for brain health.
    • Belief and tradition: that a specific number (like 528 Hz) repairs DNA, or that 432 Hz is "in tune with the universe". These ideas have meaning for many people, but controlled studies have not shown that one exact frequency heals better than another pleasant sound.
    • What clearly matters: your intention, your breathing, the volume (soft), the timbre (smooth sine and pad sounds), slow movement in the sound, and how long you listen. Build sessions with those in mind and trust what you notice in your own body.

    Sound is a complement to medical care, never a replacement. If you are working with people who have epilepsy, pulsing sounds (and flashing lights) should be used with care.

    8. Notes and their frequencies

    Each semitone multiplies the frequency by the twelfth root of 2 (about 1.0595). Tap any cell to hear it. Switch the tuning to compare A = 440 Hz (the world standard) with A = 432 Hz.

    9. Protect your ears

    • Your ears are your career. Mix at a moderate level (around the volume of normal conversation) and take a 10-minute break every hour.
    • Pure tones above about 8 kHz and below 60 Hz are deceptive: they can be loud and damaging before they sound loud. Start low and turn up slowly.
    • Ringing (tinnitus) or dull hearing after a session is a warning sign. Rest your ears.
    • Headphones for binaural beats: keep the volume low; the effect does not need loudness.
